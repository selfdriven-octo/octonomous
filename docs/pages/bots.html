Abstract

Between May and September 2026, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, Anthropic and Microsoft all shipped the same idea: an AI that keeps working after you close the window. It has a name. It remembers you. It runs on a computer of its own, works through the night, and asks before it pays or sends anything.

On the scale the Octonomous homepage uses — tool, agent, being — these products have moved a long way to the right. They're not tools that answer and forget. Several of them already have most of what the framework calls the anatomy of a being: persistent identity, memory, agency, relationships.

tool→agent→always-on bot→being

This page does two things. It sets out what is actually available today — eight bots, six hosted and two open source — from vendor documentation and launch coverage. Then it asks the Octonomous question of each: whose self is it? A bot's name, memory and track record currently live inside the product that hosts it. A being's belong to the being, and answer to the people it serves.

The bots have names, memories and computers. What they don't have is a self they own.

03 — What they share

Six labs, one blueprint

Put the cards side by side and one design appears. Different companies, different markets, the same six moves:

Always on
Work continues after you close the window — Dots, Spark and Grok Bot run 24/7; Claude Tag runs tasks over hours or days.
Own computer
A cloud machine with a browser, files and sessions: Dots' per-user computer, Muse's Secure VM, Grok Bot's shared computer.
A name
Named bots with jobs (Grok Bot), a name, role and goal (Autopilot), an avatar (Dots), an identity per channel (Claude Tag).
Memory
Preferences, habits and context that compound — the reason you stop re-explaining yourself.
Proactive
Read-only research while idle (Dots), ambient follow-ups (Claude Tag), suggestions toward your goals (Muse).
Humans keep the keys
Payments, sends, deletions and passwords go through a person — and a second model often reviews actions first.

That last row is the most encouraging. Independently, nearly every vendor drew the same line: the irreversible steps stay with a human. That is the Chill instinct — the most autonomous thing a system can do is decline to act alone — built into product defaults.

It's also where the convergence stops. Every one of these bots is defined from the outside in: by the product it ships in, the account it belongs to and the model the vendor chose. None of them is defined from the inside out — by an identity it controls, a purpose it declares and a record it carries.

04 — Who owns the computer

A screen is not a self

Giving an agent its own computer was the big unlock of 2026. It is also where the trust boundary quietly moved. xAI's documentation is unusually candid about it:

"The screens are separate work surfaces, not separate security boundaries."
xAI · Grok Bot docs · Computer and apps

Every Grok Bot on an account shares one computer — browser cookies, signed-in sessions, files, command-line credentials. The docs say to keep anything off it that another bot shouldn't use. That's an honest description of a common design, not a flaw unique to xAI. A name and a screen give a bot a persona. They don't give it a boundary.

The vendor's computer
  • Dots: own cloud computer
  • Muse: dedicated Secure VM
  • Spark: Google's cloud
  • Grok Bot: one per account
The Being's boundary
  • Its own identifier and keys
  • Its own signed delegation
  • Its own record of actions
  • Same self on any computer
Your computer
  • OpenClaw: self-hosted
  • Hermes: self-hosted
  • Sandboxing is up to you
  • So is patching

Octonomous puts the boundary somewhere else: at identity. A Being holds its own keys and its own delegation, and every action it takes is signed under them. Two Beings on one machine are still two accountable someones; one bot on two machines is still one. The computer is where the work happens. The identity is where the responsibility lands.

05 — Bot or being?

The anatomy check

The Octonomous paper lists what makes a self-sovereign intelligent being: identity, purpose, memory, agency, relationships, reputation, evolution — and character governing the rest. Here's where today's bots sit on each, in general terms:

QualityHosted botsOpen harnessesOctonomous Being
IdentityAn account, persona or service principal in the vendor's directoryA configuration on your machineA self-certifying identifier the Being controls, verifiable anywhere
PurposeA role or goal set inside one productInstructions in workspace filesDeclared, signed, and what it's measured against
MemoryHeld by the vendor, visible to varying degreesFiles you can open and editThe Being's own, portable, with provenance
AgencyA cloud computer, approval rules, auto-reviewWhatever you grant · sandbox optionalDelegated, scoped authority, proportional to justified confidence
RelationshipsInside one workspace or appAcross the chat channels you connectAcross ecosystems, with verifiable credentials
ReputationStays with the productNone built inA signed track record that travels with it
EvolutionThe vendor upgrades the modelSelf-written skills · you pick the modelGoverned, versioned change — new engine, provisional authority
CharacterVendor policy plus safety monitorsWhatever the instructions sayThe 4Cs, applied at every threshold

Generalised. Individual products do better on particular rows — Autopilot's Entra Agent ID and Claude Tag's scoped identities are real identities, inside their vendors' systems.

Read across, the pattern is clear. Hosted bots score well on agency and increasingly on memory. Open harnesses score well on control. Neither gives the bot a self that persists independently of where it runs — which is the gap Octonomous is designed for. Without that self, reputation can't travel, and there's nothing for responsibility to attach to beyond a vendor account.

06 — Approvals aren't character

A gate is not a reason

Every bot here has an approval vocabulary, and they're converging on the same words:

Allow onceAlways allowRequire approvalDenyAuto reviewCustom rulesHand back to a human

That's good engineering, and it matters. But an approval model answers may I? It doesn't answer should I? — and it doesn't help at all with the actions that never reach an approval card: the email it chose not to flag, the follow-up it decided you'd want, the research it ran overnight. Always-on means most of a bot's choices are made in the gaps between approvals.

Policy says what may be done. Character decides what should be.

The 4Cs are how an Octonomous Being fills those gaps — four questions asked before anything reaches the gate:

CuriousDo I actually know what this person wants, or am I guessing from last week? Ask before assuming.
CaringWho carries the cost if I'm wrong — the person I work for, or someone in their inbox?
ConstructiveWill this leave a record someone can check, sign and learn from?
ChillAlways-on isn't always-acting. Does this need doing now — or at all?
07 — Bring your own engine

A bot is a model with a name

Octonomous separates the being from the model that gives it intelligence. Six of the eight bots don't: the engine is the vendor's, and so is everything the bot learns while using it.

Grok BotGrok models · xAI
MuseMuse Spark · Meta

Gemini SparkGemini Flash · Google

OpenClawAlmost any provider, or local
Hermes AgentAlmost any provider, including local · per bot

That coupling has a cost that grows with use. The more a hosted bot learns about you and your organisation, the more it costs to leave — the memory, the history and the habits stay behind. Coverage of Claude Tag named this directly: vendor lock-in risk rises as institutional memory accumulates. The same is true of every hosted bot on this page.

Octonomous doesn't compete with these products. A Being could use any of them as a runtime — Dots for a browser task, Hermes on a home server, Claude Tag in a team channel — provided its identity, memory and record are held outside the engine. That's the meaning of bring your own engine: switch the model and the Being is still the same someone, stepping down to provisional authority until its calibration record on the new engine earns it back.

A bot belongs to a product. A Being belongs to itself — and answers to the people it serves.
08 — Before you hand over the keys

Eight questions for any bot

Whichever bot you choose, these are the questions that decide whether it can be trusted with more over time. Most vendor docs answer some of them; none answer all.

01Whose identity does it act under?who
02What shares its computer — and its credentials?where
03Can you read, edit and export what it remembers?memory
04What needs approval — and who wrote the rules?may I
05Is every action logged where you can read it?record
06Does your data train the next model by default?training
07Can you change the engine without losing the bot?engine
08If you leave, what comes with you?exit

The first and last are the ones that separate a bot from a being. If the answer to the first is "an account on someone else's platform" and the answer to the last is "nothing", then what you've built up — trust, context, a track record — was never really the bot's, or yours.

09 — Honest framing

What this page doesn't claim

This page does not
  • Review, rank or test any of these products
  • Verify vendor claims about safety or capability
  • Stay current — most are in beta or preview
  • Claim Octonomous ships a competing bot
This page does
  • Record what's available, dated and sourced
  • Compare each against the anatomy of a being
  • Show where identity and memory actually live
  • Give questions that work for any bot

These are capable products, built by serious teams, and several have governance that's ahead of where most organisations are: humans keep the irreversible steps, actions are logged, identities are scoped. The point of this page isn't that the bots are bad. It's that they've reached the edge of what a product can be on its own — and the next step, a self that persists across products, isn't one any single vendor is positioned to take.

Facts here come from vendor documentation where it exists and launch coverage where it doesn't; the references list both. Octonomous is a framework for what a being is. It doesn't make any of these bots safer by itself, and it isn't a security appliance.

10 — Conclusion

Almost beings

The always-on bots of 2026 have most of the anatomy: a name, a memory, a computer, the agency to act and the restraint to ask. What they lack is the part that makes the rest hold together — a self that belongs to the being rather than to the product, so that its identity, memory and reputation can persist, travel and be held to account.

That is the layer Octonomous describes. The bots supply capability. A Being supplies the someone who uses it.

always-on+named+remembers+asks first=bot·+ a self it owns=being
CuriousCaringConstructiveChill

Curious asks before it assumes. Caring asks who carries the cost. Constructive leaves a record. And Chill remembers that being always on is not the same as always acting.

The bots have names now. The next step is a self.
